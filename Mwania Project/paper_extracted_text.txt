Introduction
RNA interference (RNAi) is a sequence-specific gene silencing mechanism found in
plants, fungi and insects, playing vital roles in defense against RNA virus infection
[1,2]. There are three main small silencing RNA pathways in insects, including the
microRNA (miRNA) pathway, the Piwi-interacting RNA (piRNA) pathway, and the
small interfering RNA (siRNA) pathway according to different types of small RNAs
(sRNAs). These three small silencing RNA pathways play distinct but interconnected
roles in insect biology, with siRNA mainly for antiviral defense, miRNA for gene
regulation, and piRNA for transposon silencing [3]. miRNAs are a class of endoge-
nous sRNAs with a length of about 22 nucleotides (nt), which play various essential
regulatory roles in gene expression via degradation or translational repression of
mRNAs [4,5]. The piRNA pathway is crucial for protecting the genome against the
activity of transposable elements in animals, generating a class of piRNA of the size
25–30 nt, which regulates mRNA stability, protein synthesis, chromatin organization
and genome structure by binding with PIWI clade of Argonaute proteins [6,7]. siR-
NAs are approximately 20–25 nt small RNAs derived from double-stranded RNA that
mediate gene silencing by guiding the degradation of complementary single-stranded
transcripts [8,9].

Previous studies revealed that the miRNA pathway plays a vital role in insect development [10–13], while piRNAs
mainly function in germ cells [14–18]. In Hemiptera insects, silencing the miRNA pathway core genes (AGO1 or DCR1)
reduced miRNA levels followed by reduced fecundity and survival of Bemisia tabaci, Sogatella furcifera, Nilaparvata
lugens and Aphis gossypii [13,19–21]. It has been revealed that piRNA pathway mediates antiviral defense in mosqui-
toes but not in fruit flies although both of them are Dipteran insects. Knockdown of Piwi4 has been shown to enhance the
replication of semliki forest virus in Aedes aegypti mosquito cells (Aag2) [16]. Piwi5 and AGO3 were demonstrated to be
essential for piRNA biogenesis from sindbis virus (SINV) in infected Aag2 cells [17]. In contrast to A. aegypti, the piRNA
pathway is not required for antiviral defense in Drosophila melanogaster [18]. Additionally, endogenous viral elements
have been reported to be widespread in arthropod genomes and can also produce piRNAs [22]. In the small brown plan-
thopper (Laodelphax striatellus), endogenous toti-like viral elements (LsETLVEs) are ubiquitously integrated into the host
genome and produce piRNAs ranging from 24 to 29 nt in length [23].
Although the siRNA pathway is widely regarded as the primary antiviral immune mechanism in insects, most studies
have been conducted in model Dipteran insects, particularly fruit flies [24–29] and mosquitoes [30–33]. During virus infec-
tion, Dicer-2 (DCR2) recognizes virus-derived dsRNA and cleaves it into siRNA duplexes. The siRNAs are then integrated
into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), where Argonaute-2 (AGO2) binds to the guide strand and mediates
cleavage of the target RNA, while the passenger strand is discarded and degraded by a nuclease C3PO [34]. The DCR2-
R2D2 heterodimer plays critical roles in both siRNA production and the loading of siRNAs onto AGO2. R2D2 contains two
dsRNA-binding domains (RBD1 and RBD2). In Drosophila, dsRNA binds to the helicase domain of DCR2, whereas siRNA
binds to R2D2 and is subsequently loaded onto AGO2 with the aid of the Hsc70/Hsp90 chaperone machinery [35]. Mutant
flies lacking AGO2 were more susceptible to viral infections with Drosophila C virus and cricket paralysis virus (CrPV).
Moreover, Dicer2 mutant flies were hypersensitive to CrPV infection [36,37]. R2D2 forms a heterodimer with DCR2 in vivo,
which is required for siRNA loading into RISC, and R2D2 mutant flies also exhibit enhanced disease susceptibility to CrPV
[37]. In mosquitoes, viral titers and infection rates of SINV and dengue virus were significantly altered in AGO2 and DCR2
deficient insects [31,38,39]. Beyond flies and mosquitoes, siRNA-mediated antiviral immunity has also been systematically
studied in non-model arthropods, such as sand flies [40], Asian longhorned ticks [41,42], and shrimp [43].
Virus-derived small interfering RNAs (vsiRNAs) are key components of the siRNA-based antiviral response in
insects. In Dipteran insects, vsiRNAs typically exhibit a 21-nt peak and are generated through DCR2-mediated
cleavage [44]. Interestingly, previous studies have reported variations in vsiRNA peak lengths across different insect
orders [45], implying potential differences in their production mechanisms mediated by siRNA pathway of various
host insects. For example, vsiRNA peak is at 20-nt in Lepidopteran insects [46], 22-nt in Hymenopteran insects [47],
and both 21- and 22-nt in Hemipteran insects [48,49]. Additionally, DCR2 has also been found to inhibit molting and
regulate wing expansion in S. furcifera [50], indicating possible functional diversity of DCR2 besides mediating antivi-
ral RNAi in insects.
Our current understanding of insect small silencing RNA pathways primarily relies on studies of mosquitoes and flies,
while mechanisms of small silencing RNA pathways in non-model insects, particularly during viral infection, remain poorly
understood. The three planthopper species L. striatellus, N. lugens, and S. furcifera (family Delphacidae, order Hemip-
tera) are among the most destructive insect pests in rice fields. In addition to transmitting the economically important rice
viruses (such as rice stripe virus, RSV), our recent investigations reveal that L. striatellus also harbors a diverse array of
symbiotic viruses or insect-specific RNA viruses [49]. To elucidate roles of RNAi in hemipteran insects, this study system-
atically investigates the pivotal functions of key small silencing RNA pathways genes in L. striatellus. Notably, we found
that AGO2 depletion consistently enhanced viral accumulation, whereas DCR2 depletion did not produce a detectable
antiviral phenotype under the conditions examined. Similar contrasting knockdown phenotypes for AGO2 and DCR2 were
observed in three other hemipteran insects (N. lugens, S. furcifera, and B. tabaci). Importantly, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated
DCR2 knockout in L. striatellus yielded homozygous mutant embryos that failed to hatch, indicating an essential role for

Results
Identification of key small silencing RNA pathway genes in the three rice planthoppers
Based on the homologous search to the genes in model insects (D. melanogaster and A. aegypti), 21 core small silenc-
ing RNA pathway genes (AGO1, DCR1, Piwi, AGO3, AGO2, DCR2 and R2D2) were identified in three rice planthoppers,
among which 11 genes were newly identified, including Ls_DCR1 (accession no. OP778735), Ls_Piwi (accession no.
OP778736), Ls_AGO3 (accession no. OP778737), Ls_R2D2 (accession no. OP778738) of L. striatellus and Sf_AGO1
(accession no. OP778739), Sf_DCR1 (accession no. OP778741), Sf_Piwi (accession no. OP778744), Sf_AGO3 (acces-
sion no. OP778745), Sf_AGO2 (accession no. OP778740), Sf_DCR2 (accession no. OP778742), and Sf_R2D2 (acces-
sion no. OP778743) of S. furcifera. Phylogenetic analysis based on protein sequences of the small silencing RNA pathway
core genes in L. striatellus revealed that most genes (except R2D2) clustered closely with those of S. furcifera. Together
with N. lugens, they formed distinct clades separate from those of other species (Fig 1), consistent with the taxonomic
relationships among the three species [51]. Domain analysis suggested that most genes of the three rice planthoppers
possess conserved domains of the three small silencing RNA pathways core genes similar to other insects (Fig 1). It is
worth noting that Ls_DCR1 lacks the DEAD/DEAH helicase_C (DHC) domain (Fig 1A), Ls_R2D2 has an additional dsRNA
binding (dRB) motif (Fig 1B) compared to their counterparts in other insects, suggesting potential functional differences of
these genes.
Effects of key small silencing RNA pathway genes on host phenotypes of L. striatellus
To investigate the roles of the three small silencing RNA pathways on host biology, we performed RNAi-mediated knock-
down of key small silencing RNA-related genes in L. striatellus and evaluated their effects on host fecundity, survival
rate, and developmental duration. For the miRNA pathway, both Ls_AGO1 and Ls_DCR1 were expressed across the
detected tissues of L. striatellus, with Ls_AGO1 showing the lowest expression in the testis and Ls_DCR1 showing the
highest expression in both reproductive organs (ovary and testis) (Fig 2A-a). Relatively high expression of Ls_AGO1 was
observed in eggs and early nymphal stages (S1A Fig), while extremely high expression of Ls_DCR1 was only detected
in early stage of egg development (12h) (S1B Fig). After knockdown of Ls_AGO1 and Ls_DCR1 in third-instar nymphs
(silencing efficiency shown in S2A Fig), the survival rate of L. striatellus was significantly reduced 12 days post-
injection compared to the ds-GFP control group (Fig 2A-b). Additionally, treated nymphs exhibited molting defects,
whereas most control nymphs successfully molted into adults. The defect was manifested by nymphs remaining in their
original molting stage, with the old exoskeleton failing to shed (Fig 2A-b). Female fecundity was significantly reduced
in both ds-Ls_AGO1 and ds-Ls_DCR1 treated insect groups compared to the ds-GFP control. Knockdown of Ls_DCR1
completely abolished egg production, while Ls_AGO1 knockdown led to a limited number of eggs, all of which failed to
hatch (Fig 2A-c; S3 Fig; S1 Table).
For the piRNA pathway, both Ls_Piwi and Ls_AGO3 of L. striatellus were highly expressed in the ovary, followed
by the testis (Fig 2B-a). High expression of Ls_Piwi and Ls_AGO3 were observed in early stages of egg development
(S1C-D Fig). The survival rates of the ds-Ls_Piwi and ds-Ls_AGO3 treated insect groups (silencing efficiency shown
in S2B Fig) were not significantly different from those of the control group at 12 days post injection. Most of the insect

curls (Fig 2B-b). Additionally, ds-Ls_Piwi injection severely reduced the production of eggs in adult L. striatellus (Fig 2B-c)
and all of the eggs failed to hatch (S3 Fig and S1 Table). Similarly, decreased production of eggs was also observed for

miRNA and piRNA produced by corresponding pathways in L. striatellus
In the miRNA pathway, DCR1 and AGO1 play essential roles in producing functional miRNAs (~22 nt long) and executing gene
silencing [52]. To explore the functions of miRNA pathway in L. striatellus, knockdown of Ls_AGO1 or Ls_DCR1 in third-instar
nymphs was performed by injecting their corresponding dsRNAs. Compared to the control group (ds-GFP), the transcriptional
expression of Ls_AGO1 and Ls_DCR1 were successfully inhibited with decreased level of 82% and 70%, respectively (Fig 3A-
a). Our results showed that miRNA counts of the length 22 nt in the ds-GFP group were approximately 2 times and 1.5 times
higher than those in the ds-Ls_AGO1 and ds-Ls_DCR1 treated groups, respectively (Fig 3A-b), suggesting that both Ls_AGO1
and Ls_DCR1 play a crucial role in the production of miRNAs similar to previous report in D. melanogaster [52].
The piRNA pathway is essential for the development and differentiation of animal germ cells [7,15,53], and has also
been recently implicated in antiviral defense, particularly in mosquito cells [16]. To determine the functional role of piRNA
pathway in L. striatellus, the core genes of this pathway, Ls_Piwi and Ls_AGO3, were efficiently inhibited using dsRNA
injection approach in third-instar nymphs (Fig 3B-a). sRNA sequencing analysis revealed that the abundance of piRNA-
sized reads (26–30 nt) was significantly reduced in insects treated with ds-Ls_Piwi or ds-Ls_AGO3 compared to the
ds-GFP control group (Fig 3B-b). Since piRNAs of L. striatellus are also produced from LsETLVEs as described previously
[23], this type of piRNAs were subsequently analyzed. We found that piRNA reads derived from LsETLVEs (LsETLVE1,
LsETLVE4, LsETLVE5, LsETLVE6, and LsETLVE9; the names and the sequences are derived from reference [23])
were also dramatically reduced in ds-Ls_Piwi treated insect group compared to the control (Fig 3B_c-g). These results
demonstrate that Ls_Piwi is actively involved in production of piRNA-sized reads in L. striatellus. Although knockdown of
Ls_AGO3 did not significantly reduce abundance of EVE-derived piRNA-sized reads under our experimental conditions,
we cannot rule out a role for Ls_AGO3 in the piRNA pathway, as residual protein activity or limited ping-pong amplification
in this species may have masked a detectable piRNA phenotype.

AGO2 depletion, but not DCR2 or R2D2 depletion, enhances viral accumulation in L. striatellus
In insects, the siRNA pathway is widely recognized as a primary antiviral defense mechanism against RNA virus infection,
and has been comprehensively characterized in model dipteran insects (e.g., Drosophila and mosquitoes) [8,33]. To eval-
uate the antiviral role of siRNA pathway in hemipteran insect, we efficiently knocked down three key genes in third-instar

results indicated that significant increase of viral abundance and corresponding vsiRNA reads for the four ISVs (LSSolV2,

also investigated. Distribution patterns of four ISVs derived vsiRNA suggested that both the core genes of miRNA pathway

showed no significant difference compared to the corresponding GFP controls (S6 Fig).
To test potential functional redundancy, we performed double and triple knockdown experiments by simultaneously
injecting dsRNAs targeting Ls_DCR1/Ls_DCR2 (double knockdown) and Ls_DCR2/Ls_AGO2/Ls_R2D2 (triple knock-

under the tested knockdown conditions. In the triple knockdown group (ds-Ls_DCR2/AGO2/R2D2), a significant increase
of viral abundance and corresponding vsiRNA reads was observed for the three ISVs (S7 Fig), driven by the inclusion of

consistently increased viral accumulation, whereas depletion of DCR1, DCR2, or R2D2 did not produce comparable anti-

controls under both knockdown conditions. We further analyzed total clean small RNA reads (18–30 nt), and the overall

additional population of reads within the 25–30 nt range (S8 Fig).

Discussion
The three conserved small silencing RNA pathways - miRNA, piRNA, and siRNA - are essential for host biology and
antiviral defense, as has been comprehensively demonstrated in dipteran insects, particularly in mosquitoes and fruit
flies [3]. However, their functions remain largely unexplored in non-model insects. This study functionally compared core
components of the three major small-RNA pathways in L. striatellus. We found that AGO2 depletion consistently enhanced
the accumulation of multiple RNA viruses, whereas comparable antiviral phenotypes were not detected after depletion of
DCR2 or R2D2. Similar contrasting knockdown responses were observed in three additional hemipteran species. These
findings reveal unexpected functional divergence among conserved siRNA-pathway components in hemipteran insects
and highlight the need to determine how antiviral vsiRNAs are generated in this lineage.
In insects, the miRNA pathway plays a crucial role in maintaining physiological homeostasis by regulating processes
such as development, metamorphosis, immune responses, and reproduction [11,12]. In this study, knockdown of either
Ls_AGO1 or Ls_DCR1 of L. striatellus resulted in a noticeable reduction of 22-nt miRNAs compared to GFP control
(Fig 3A). Additionally, suppression of Ls_AGO1 or Ls_DCR1 significantly affected the survival rate, ecdysis, and reproduc-
tion of L. striatellus (Fig 2A). Similarly, recent studies demonstrated that depletion of AGO1 or DCR1 disrupted survival,
molting, and reproduction in three other hemipteran insects, N. lugens, S. furcifera, and B. tabaci [13,20,21,57], suggest-
ing that the miRNA pathway is conserved in hemipterans during key physiological process.
The piRNA pathway mainly plays a role in germ cells, and Piwi mutations in female and male Drosophila cannot renew
germline stem cells, leading to germline degeneration and sterility [15,58]. AGO3 mutant female flies have been reported
to be sterile, with homozygous AGO3 mutant females laying fewer eggs and failing to hatch compared to wild-type or
heterozygous females [14]. In L. striatellus, our results showed that adult fecundity and the egg hatchability were severely
affected compared to the control when Ls_Piwi and Ls_AGO3 were silenced (Fig 2B-c, S3 Fig, and S1 Table). A recent
report also revealed that Ago3 and Piwi of another rice planthopper (N. lugens) significantly disrupt female reproductive
processes [59], indicating their essential roles in germ cell development of insects. Interestingly, we found that the abun-
dance of EVE piRNA-sized reads (26–29 nt) was significantly reduced in L. striatellus only when Ls_Piwi was silenced, but
not when Ls_AGO3 was targeted (Fig 3B). In contrast, in N. lugens, silencing either of Piwi or Ago3 completely eliminated
the piRNA peak [59], highlighting the divergent roles of these proteins across hemipteran insects. The lack of a detectable
piRNA-sized reads reduction upon Ls_AGO3 knockdown should be interpreted cautiously because it may reflect incom-
plete protein depletion or low baseline ping-pong amplification activity in L. striatellus rather than a genuine functional
absence of AGO3 in the piRNA pathway.
Although all the three major small silencing RNA pathways (miRNA, piRNA, and siRNA) contribute to combating RNA
virus infections, the siRNA pathway is generally considered as the primary one in insects, with three key genes - AGO2,
DCR2, and R2D2 - playing critical roles in this process [16,17,44,60]. Unexpectedly, knockdown experiments revealed
that only repression of Ls_AGO2, but not Ls_DCR2 or Ls_R2D2, resulted in a significant increase for the accumulation

of both viral mRNA and vsiRNA compared to control in L. striatellus infected with four ISVs (LSSolV2, LSSolV3, LSIfV2,
and LSHV1) (Fig 4B) and a plant virus (RSV) (S5C Fig). It should also be noted that the peak length of vsiRNAs (21- and
22-nt) was maintained in ds-Ls_DCR2 and ds-Ls_R2D2 treated insects compared to control. In dipteran insects, DCR2
is an RNase III enzyme that recognizes and cleaves dsRNA of viral origin into 21-nt siRNAs [29,34]. In DCR2-null sib-
ling mosquitoes (A. aegypti), the abundance of 21-nt vsiRNA was significantly reduced and the 21-nt length peak also
disappeared compared to the wild mosquitoes. Moreover, the insect was acutely susceptible to both arbovirus and the
insect-specific alphavirus in DCR2 mutant mosquitoes, suggesting the critical roles of DCR2 in siRNA-mediated antivi-
ral defense [61–63]. To account for differences in viral RNA abundance among treatment groups, we further normalized
the raw vsiRNA read counts to the corresponding viral RNA copy numbers determined by absolute qPCR quantification.
Following normalization, the reads/copies ratio was significantly reduced in Ls_AGO2-knockdown insects, whereas no sig-
nificant difference was observed following Ls_DCR2 or Ls_R2D2 knockdown compared with the control (S4 Fig). Together
with the preservation of canonical vsiRNA size profiles and strand polarity following Ls_DCR2 knockdown, these results
indicate that Ls_DCR2 depletion did not detectably alter vsiRNA accumulation relative to viral RNA abundance under the
tested conditions. Moreover, the RNAi-of-RNAi assay using a non-replicating reporter substrate provided complementary
evidence that Ls_DCR2 knockdown did not detectably impair RNAi activity.
“RNAiof RNAi” assays indicated that pre-knockdown of Ls_AGO2 significantly impaired cleavage of subsequently
injected ds-Ls_Tubulin, whereas pre-knockdown of Ls_DCR1 or Ls_DCR2 had no significant effect, demonstrating
that DCR2 pre-knockdown did not detectably alter reporter silencing, but residual DCR2 activity could not be excluded
in L. striatellus (S9 Fig). On the contrary, in Locusta migratoria (Orthoptera), silencing of Lm-DCR1, Lm-DCR2a and
Lm-DCR2b prior to injection of ds-Lm-Tubulin diminished RNAi efficiency for Lm-Tubulin, suggesting that all the three
Lm-Dicers contribute to RNAi [64]. Similarly, RNAi-related factor in Schistocerca gregaria (Orthoptera), Tribolium (Cole-
optera), Leptinotarsa decemlineata (Coleoptera), and Chilo suppressalis (Lepidoptera) can also affect RNAi efficiency
[65–68]. These findings differ from our knockdown observations in L. striatellus, raising the possibility that antiviral siRNA
pathway organization may vary among insect lineages. However, broader taxonomic sampling and direct genetic analyses
will be required to determine whether the observed phenotype is widespread within Hemiptera. Additionally, we observed
typical vsiRNA profiles in wild-type planthoppers that are consistent with processing by a Dicer-family enzyme, raising
the possibility that another nuclease, residual DCR2 activity, or an alternative processing mechanism may contribute
to vsiRNA production in L. striatellus. Identifying a putative alternative nuclease would represent an important direction
for future research. To further confirm the function of Ls_DCR2, CRISPR/Cas9 was performed to knockout DCR2 in L.
striatellus. Unexpectedly, we failed to obtain homozygous mutant insects in the G2 generations because the loss of DCR2
caused insect embryonic lethality (Fig 5). Furthermore, a significant extension in the duration of nymph development
stages (1st and 5th) was observed for the two heterozygous mutants compared to WT insects (S10 Fig), demonstrating
an essential developmental role for DCR2, while its antiviral contribution could not be directly assessed in homozygous
mutants of L. striatellus. Similarly, a previous report suggested that AGO2 and DCR2 of L. striatellus do not actively
respond to RSV infection although AGO2 knockdown resulted in increased viral RNA levels [69]. Additionally, interference
of DCR2 resulted in the disrupting of the molting and wing expansion in S. furcifera [50]. Furthermore, to elucidate the
antiviral roles of AGO2 and DCR2 in hemipteran insects beyond L. striatellus, we investigated species from two different
genera (Nilaparvata and Sogatella) of the family Delphacidae, as well as from another family, Aleyrodidae. Consistent with
the results in L. striatellus, AGO2 depletion, but not DCR2 depletion, produced a detectable increase in viral accumulation
in all three additional species examined (Fig 6). Given the high abundance of vsiRNAs generated in hemipteran insects,
their biogenesis may involve residual DCR2 activity, another nuclease, or an alternative processing mechanism that
remains to be identified. Collectively, our findings reveal a reproducible contrast between AGO2- and DCR2-knockdown
phenotypes in the four hemipteran species examined. These observations suggest that the canonical DCR2-centered
model derived from dipteran insects may not fully explain the measured antiviral RNAi responses in these species. Future

studies aimed at identifying the nuclease responsible for antiviral vsiRNA biogenesis, or conditionally eliminating DCR2
activity, will be essential for resolving the organization of antiviral RNAi pathways in hemipteran insects.
Materials and methods
Insect cultures
The three rice planthopper species, L. striatellus, N. lugens and S. furcifera, were reared in the laboratory, separately on
seedlings of rice (Oryza sativa Xiushui) in a growth chamber at 26 ± 1°C, 80% relative humidity, and a 14 h light/10 h dark
photoperiod. The whitefly B. tabaci (MED) was originally collected from Ningbo, Zhejiang province and maintained on
cotton plants in the laboratory at Ningbo University.
RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was isolated from insects (approximately 15 planthoppers or a single whitefly) using the RNAiso Plus (Takara,
Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality of RNA was detected using a NanoDrop 2000 spectro-
photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Total RNA was subjected to reverse transcription to complementary DNA
(cDNA) based on HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) (Vazyme, China). Quantitative real-time PCR
(RT-qPCR) was performed on a LightCycler Real-Time PCR System (Roche, Switzerland) with Hieff qPCR SYBR Green
Master Mix (Low Rox Plus) (Yeasen, China). The expression level of each gene in L. striatellus was normalized to the
housekeeping gene ACTIN and was calculated with the 2-∆∆Ct method. Each experiment was performed with three biologi-
cal replicates, and two technical replicates were set up for each biological replicate.
Phylogenetic analysis
The predicted protein sequences of key small silencing RNA pathway genes of L. striatellus were aligned with those of
other insect species (D. melanogaster, Tribolium castaneum, Caenorhabditis elegans, Apis florea, N. lugens, and S.
furcifera) which were retrieved from NCBI protein database. The conserved domains of these genes were determined
with NCBI CD-Search tools (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) and their genome structures were
then visualized by TBtools [70]. Phylogenetic analysis was constructed based on their full-length protein sequences using
MEGA 6 software with maximum likelihood method.
Double-stranded RNA synthesis and injection
dsRNAs of the key RNAi-related genes (L. striatellus: AGO1, AGO2, DCR1, DCR2, R2D2, Piwi, AGO3; N. lugens: AGO2, DCR2;
S. furcifera: AGO2, DCR2; B. tabaci: AGO2, DCR2) and GFP were synthesized with T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (Vazyme,
China) in line with the manufacturer’s protocol. Primers (with T7 promoter sequences) for these genes are shown in S3 Table. The
quality of synthesized dsRNA was determined using agarose gel electrophoresis. The volumes of dsRNA (500 ng/μL) injected
into 1st-, 3rd-instar nymphs and one-day-old adults were approximately 10 nL, 25 nL, 50 nL, respectively. dsRNA was injected into
hemolymph through ventral thorax of the insect using a glass needle which was operated with a TransferMan 4r micromanipulator
(Eppendorf, Germany). After injection, the nymphs were placed on fresh rice seedlings grown in glass tubes 5 cm in diameter.
Injection of virus crude extracts
Approximately 10 RSV-infected adult insects were homogenized with toothpicks in 100 μL of 10 mM PBS (pH 7.4) in a 1.5
mL EP tube. After centrifugation at 8,000 rpm at 4°C for 5 min, the supernatant was retained. The process was repeated
three times and the supernatant of the last centrifugation was used as the viral crude extracts for injection. A total of 25
nL of viral crude extracts was injected into the hemolymph of 3rd-instar insect nymphs and then transferred to fresh rice
seedlings for rearing.

Fecundity, survival and developmental duration
To investigate the functions of key small silencing RNA-related genes in planthopper, corresponding dsRNAs were
injected into one-day-old (newly emerged) females and males of L. striatellus. Then, one female and two male insects
were transferred into a glass tube with a rice seedling for three days. Subsequently, the males were removed and the
female was maintained in the tube for another ten days before removal. To determine hatch ratio of the planthopper eggs,
rice seedlings were dissected ten days after removal of the females, and the number of unhatched eggs was recorded.
The number of effective groups for each gene is at least 19 for this experiment. Moreover, to investigate the effects of
these genes on the insect survival, 3rd-instar nymphs of L. striatellus were used for dsRNA injection. Three groups were
set up for each gene, with approximately 100 insects per group. Two days after injection, the surviving insects were
recorded daily for next 12 days. Additionally, to evaluate the effects of the genes on insect developmental stages, the
1st-instar nymphs were injected with corresponding dsRNA. Each nymph was individually maintained in a glass tube and
the developmental stage was recorded at 12-hour intervals until natural death. The number of effective groups for each
gene is at least 20 for this experiment. Gene silencing efficiency was assessed in approximately 30 insects at two days
post dsRNA injection.
Tissue- and developmental stage-specific expression patterns of key RNAi genes in L. striatellus
To investigate tissue-specific expression patterns of small silencing RNA-related genes in L. striatellus, different tissues
were dissected from 3-day-old females and males, including carcass, fat body, gut, salivary glands, ovary and testis. For
the developmental expression patterns, egg (12h, 24h, 48h, 96h, 144h, 168h), 1st-instar nymphs (0h, 12h, 24h), 2nd-instar
nymphs (0h, 12h, 24h, 36h), 3rd-instar nymphs (0h, 12h, 24h, 36h, 48h, 60h), 4th-instar nymphs (0h, 12h, 24h, 36h,48h,
60h), 5th-instar nymphs (0h, 12h, 24h, 36h, 48h, 60h, 72h), female and male adults (0h, 12h, 24h, 48h, 96h, 144h, 192h)
were used. Then the RNA was extracted by RNAiso Plus and the expressions were determined by qRT-PCR as described
above.
Insect-specific RNA viruses identified in the lab population of L. striatellus
Besides rice viruses, L. striatellus also harbors a large number of different ISVs [49]. To further explore the antiviral roles
of the three small silencing RNA pathways, viruses presented in the lab population of L. striatellus were investigated.
Virus screening indicated that, in addition to RSV, seven ISVs were identified in the lab population of L. striatellus through
RT-PCR detection and Sanger sequencing confirmation (S4 Table). Moreover, infection rates of these ISVs in individual
insects were evaluated. Our results demonstrated that the infection rates of these ISVs varied from 28.9% to 97.8% in the
insect population (S11 Fig). Considering that Himetobi P virus derived vsiRNAs exhibit non-canonical pattern [71], four
ISVs with infection rate higher than 60% were selected for subsequent antiviral response analysis in L. striatellus, includ-
ing Laodelphax striatellus sobeli-like virus 2 (LSSolV2, accession no. BK068174), Laodelphax striatellus sobeli-like virus
3 (LSSolV3, accession no. BK068175), Laodelphax striatellus iflavirus 2 (LSIfV2, accession no. PP781946), and Laodel-
phax striatellus honeydew virus 1 (LSHV1, accession no. NC_023627.1).
RNA-seq analysis
Total RNA was extracted from L. striatellus for transcriptome sequencing and small RNA (sRNA) sequencing. Specifically,
transcriptome sequencing was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform and sRNA was sequenced by Illumina
HiSeq 2500 platform (Novogene, Tianjin, China). For sRNA sequencing, two independent biological replicates were ana-
lyzed for each treatment. De novo assembly of transcriptome was accomplished based on clean reads from all libraries

of sequenced sRNAs, as described previously [48]. For the total small RNA size-distribution analysis shown in S8 Fig,

all quality-controlled 18–30 nt reads were grouped according to read length and normalized as reads per million (RPM).
Afterwards, sRNAs with the length of 18–30 nt were fetched and mapped into the genome sequences of the identified
viruses using Bowtie software (allowing zero mismatch). Virus-derived small RNAs (vsiRNAs) were extracted for down-
stream analyses, including size distribution and genomic mapping profiles, using Linux bash scripts. The abundance of
vsiRNAs was calculated as RPM, defined as the number of vsiRNA reads × 10⁶ divided by the total number of reads in
each sample. For the analysis shown in S4 Fig, the raw vsiRNA read count for each virus was further normalized to the
corresponding viral RNA abundance by dividing the vsiRNA read count by the viral RNA copy number determined by
absolute qPCR quantification. The resulting reads/copies ratio was used to represent vsiRNA abundance relative to viral
RNA abundance. To analyze piRNA-sized reads abundance, quality-controlled sRNA reads were mapped to the L. striatel-
lus genome or to endogenous viral element (EVE) sequences. To analyze miRNA abundance, quality-controlled sRNAs
mapped to the L. striatellus genome were sequentially aligned to the miRBase microRNA database (allowing zero mis-
matches), followed by genomic repeat sequences, mRNA sequences, intronic regions, and ncRNA databases (allowing
up to two mismatches). Small RNAs were subsequently classified and annotated using custom Python scripts, which are
publicly available in a GitHub repository (https://github.com/Zhuangxinye/vsirna-pipeline).
RNAi of RNAi assay
An RNAi of RNAi assay was conducted to assess the effect of Ls_DCR1, Ls_DCR2 and Ls_AGO2 on RNAi efficiency in
L. striatellus. Third instar nymphs were injected with dsRNA into the hemocoel. After 48 h, nymphs of the ds-Ls_DCR1,
ds-Ls_DCR2 and ds-Ls_AGO2 or ds-GFP-injected groups were re-injected with equal doses of ds-Ls_Tubulin, whereas
nymphs from an additional ds-GFP-injected group were reinjected with ds-GFP as a double-injection negative control.
24 hours after the second injection, total RNA was extracted from single L. striatellus individuals by using RNAiso Plus
(Takara, Japan) as described above, and the expression level of the Ls_Tubulin gene was analyzed.
CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of Ls_DCR2
The potential target sites for synthesizing sgRNA for Ls_DCR2 were predicted using the sgRNAcas9 algorithm 3.0.5 [73].
The search parameters were set as follows: sgRNA length of 20 nucleotides, GC content between 20–80%, and NGG as
the PAM sequence. As a result, two sgRNAs with lowest off-target possibility were selected (5’-CCTCACGTGGT
GGGGTTGAC-3’; 5’-CACTTCTGTCAACGAAAGCA-3’). PCR was performed using a forward primer containing the T7
sequence and a reverse primer containing the partial sgRNA sequence (S3 Table). The sgRNA was prepared using a T7
High Yield RNA Transcription Kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The Cas9 protein (Gen-
Script, China) and sgRNA were mixed and then microinjected using the TransferMan 4r micromanipulator (Eppendorf,
Germany). In brief, a mixture of sgRNA (300 ng/μl) and Cas9 protein (8 μg) were injected into newly deposited eggs. The
injected eggs were then transferred carefully to filter papers that were rinsed with sterilized water (containing tebuco-
nazole (20 ng/ml) and kanamycin (50 ng/ml)) and placed in an incubator at 26 ± 0.5 °C with a humidity level of 50 ± 5%. At
48 hours after injection, genomic DNA was extracted from injected eggs, and the mutation of the sgRNA target sites was
confirmed by PCR followed by Sanger sequencing. The wings of newly emerged adults (G0) were carefully detached and
used for mutation detection. Then the mutated individuals were mated with wild-type insects to produce G1. In G1, the
mutated females were mated with mutated males to produce G2 to obtain purified mutant insect strain.
Statistical analysis
All the data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics (ver. 23.0) Software. Data were shown as means ± SEM. For
pairwise comparisons, statistical significance was determined using Student’s t test. Significance was reported as *P < 0.05,
**P < 0.01. For multiple comparisons, statistical significance was determined using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA)
with Tukey’s test. For survival curves, statistical significance was determined using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.
